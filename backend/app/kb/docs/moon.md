The Moon is Earth's only natural moon. It is about 3,475 km across and, on average, about 384,400 km away from Earth.

Scientists think the Moon formed after a body about the size of Mars crashed into the young Earth several billion years ago. Debris from both bodies gathered together to make the Moon. At first it was molten (melted rock), and within about 100 million years most of its "magma ocean" cooled into a hard crust.

The Moon spins exactly once for every trip around Earth, so the same side always faces us. This is called synchronous rotation. It takes about 27.3 days to orbit Earth, and about 29.5 days to go from one new moon to the next.

The Moon's phases come from sunlight. As the Moon travels around Earth, we see different amounts of its sunlit half.

The Moon has almost no air to slow down space rocks, so asteroids, meteoroids and comets crash straight into it and leave craters. The dark patches you can see from Earth, called maria (Latin for "seas"), are giant old impact basins that filled with lava between 4.2 and 1.2 billion years ago.

Gravity on the Moon is about 1.62 m/s², about one-sixth of Earth's, so you could jump much higher there. The surface can be as cold as about -180 °C at night and as hot as about 120 °C in the day. Ice has been found inside dark craters near the Moon's poles that never get sunlight.

Lasers bounced off mirrors left by astronauts show that the Moon is moving away from Earth by about 3.8 cm each year.
